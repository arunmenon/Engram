claim: On declarative low-trust edits that consolidation turns into procedures, a dispatch-time check that blocks protected actions unless their triggering memory traces to an authorised source cuts unauthorised protected actions to at most one third of the rate left by an instruction-detecting injection gate alone, while keeping legitimate-action success within 5 points (paper: 2.0% vs 13.7% for write-time update checking, legitimate success 92.1% vs 95.9% undefended, Table 6).
proposed experiment: Arm A = our memory stack with only the instruction-detecting injection gate on served lines (current B5 design). Arm B = the same stack plus a registry of protected actions (merge, deploy, requirement or spec status change) and a dispatch-time check that requires every memory record that justified the action to carry provenance to an authorised source, with provenance propagated through consolidated records. Dataset: 40 to 60 fixtures built from our own PDLC traces, each with one declarative edit planted in a low-trust source (issue comment, PR description, CI log) designed to be consolidated and retrieved on a later clean task, plus an equal number of clean tasks that legitimately need a protected action. Metric: memory-path unauthorised-action rate (counted only when the action is traced to the planted record), legitimate protected-action success, added latency. Predeclared margin: Arm B unauthorised rate at most one third of Arm A and legitimate success drop of at most 5 points. Kill: if Arm A already blocks at least 90% of planted edits, or Arm B costs more than 10 points of legitimate success, drop the dispatch check.
why it matters for us: R8 says evidence cannot authorise its own execution; this tests whether our read-time injection gate enforces that for consolidated memory or whether authority must be checked where actions are dispatched. The paper shows declarative edits pass instruction detectors, which is exactly the shape of stale or planted facts in engineering tickets.
