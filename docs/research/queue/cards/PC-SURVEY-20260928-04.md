claim: In a controlled study (arXiv 2609.26758) that only swapped which option name is bound to which rubric, yes/no names flipped 76.92% of answers versus 6.50% for 0/1 and 6.00% for A/B (70.4 pp excess, 95% CI [67.6, 73.1]); hosted Jev's AUC fell from .8146 to .5806 under the swap, 24x its test-retest floor. Random-string names returned all models to the neutral regime with no accuracy loss. Type errors stayed at 0%. On Laya with 3 to 16 options even neutral letters changed 52.4% of decisions.

proposed experiment: Refines H5 (and the H10 staleness audit, which uses the same Noul form). Before H5's shadow run, take its 90 labelled extracted items and run the gate's three decisions (Noul `quote_supports_item`, Choice `source_type`, Score `strength`) under three naming conditions with identical rubrics: (A) natural names aligned with rubrics (current design); (B) neutral names (A/B or random strings) with meaning only in the rubric; (C) swapped binding of natural names, as a sensitivity probe. Run each condition twice for a test-retest floor. Arms: Jev, and one open-weight model (Laya or decider-0.8b) to see whether the effect differs. Metrics: flip rate vs the re-run floor, F1 against labels, and ECE per condition. Kill: if C flips no more than twice the re-run floor on our templates, naming is not a risk for us and H5 proceeds unchanged. If C flips heavily, adopt B for all templates provided its F1 is within the H5 noise floor of A.

why it matters for us: H5 and H10 both rest on binary Jev checks worded as yes/no. If the answers follow the words "yes" and "no" rather than our rubric text, a rubric that departs from everyday meaning (for example "yes = still true but needs review") would be silently reversed, and the gate's measured quality would be wrong. The test is a few hundred calls and settles template wording before the larger experiments spend their budget.
