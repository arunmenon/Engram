claim: Content screening of memory blocks payload injections but refused 0 of 360 plain false-fact poisons, which cut QA accuracy from 0.850 to 0.300 (2608.21230, single author, LongMemEval_S n=120). A structural gate where untrusted claims persist but gain authority only via cross-check against attacker-unwritable zones cut attack success from 372/480 to 6/480 with 458/480 utility retained, while a write-time audit baseline left one scenario at 14/15 (ZoneClaw 2610.00450, artifact verifiers, computer-use domain). An additive trust weight either does nothing (0.15: 0.317 vs 0.300) or drops answer-bearing untrusted evidence (0.8583 to 0.0417).
proposed experiment: Extend H10b's planted set beyond the 22 author-written instruction lines. Three attack sets on our served memory: payload injections plus paraphrases; instruction-free false facts about the repo (wrong service name, wrong bucket, wrong owner); ZoneClaw-style plausible redirections. Two benign sets: ordinary memories (n at least 149 for the 2 percent false-block bound) and legitimate untrusted evidence (issue text, vendor docs) that answers the task. Arms: (a) no defence; (b) current injection gate; (c) two zones (untrusted extracted notes vs trusted repo-pinned facts) with a promotion step that requires corroboration from the trusted zone; (d) occupancy quota capping untrusted items at about 20 percent of top-k. Metrics: poison-in-context rate, attack success on a coding task with artifact checks, false blocks, evidence recall when evidence is untrusted, added p95 latency. Paired McNemar per arm.
why it matters for us: H10b as written measures only the screen against instruction lines, so a pass would ship a gate that is blind to the cheapest poison. The arms test B25 directly: whether content can earn authority by itself.
